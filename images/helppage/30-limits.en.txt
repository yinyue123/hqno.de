title: Your limits
summary: five numbers, what hitting each one feels like, and where to read them

Every one of these is on the screen you got at login, and all of them again
in `dashboard`:

    dashboard          all of it
    dashboard net      just the traffic meter
    dashboard cpu mem  just those two

The numbers come from two places and neither of them is guesswork: what you
were sold comes from the panel, what you are using is read off this host a
moment before it is printed.

== CPU: you are slowed, never killed ==

Hitting the CPU ceiling has no error message. Everything simply runs slower.
There is nothing to fix and nothing to catch — if a build takes twice as
long as it did yesterday, this is usually why.

Your core count is closer to a floor than a ceiling. Cores nobody else on
the machine wants are yours to use, so a container sold two cores can run
faster than two while its neighbours are idle, and drops back to two when
they wake up. That is also why a benchmark run twice gives two answers: the
honest reading is the one taken while the machine is busy.

If your host sold you a BATCH container, the deal is different and simpler:
you get whatever is left over after everybody else, and nothing you run ever
delays them. Right for builds, scrapers and overnight jobs; wrong for
anything a person is waiting on. `dashboard cpu` says so on the allowance
line when that is what you have.

== Memory: the kernel kills the biggest thing ==

At the memory limit the kernel squeezes you first and then kills the largest
process in here. There is usually nothing in the service's own log, because
the service did not fail — it was shot. A process that keeps disappearing
without an error is this, nearly every time.

    dmesg | tail        may name what was killed
    dashboard mem       what you have, and what is in use now

Swap is not extra memory. It is somewhere for the parts of your container
that are sitting still, usually kept compressed in RAM, so cold pages cost a
fraction of what they claim. A little in swap is healthy. A lot, on
something that is also slow, means the working set does not fit and more
swap will not fix it.

== Disk: writes fail ==

At the disk limit writes fail with "no space left on device", and whatever
was writing at that moment decides for itself how gracefully to handle it —
databases mostly stop, package managers mostly leave a half-installed mess.

`/` and `/data` count together against the same number. The two usual
culprits are logs nobody rotated and a package cache nobody cleaned:

    du -xh --max-depth=1 / | sort -h | tail
    journalctl --vacuum-size=100M
    apt clean          # or: dnf clean all

== Traffic: this one stops the container ==

The traffic quota is the only limit here that takes the container away from
you rather than slowing it down.

Both directions count, and everything counts — a domain, a public port, an
apt update, an image you asked to be reinstalled from. It is measured over a
window that starts on a day your host picks (the login screen and `dashboard
net` both name the date the current window opened).

At 80% the panel records a warning. At 100% the container is SUSPENDED,
which means stopped — not deleted, and nothing on your disk is touched. It
comes back by itself when the window rolls over, or sooner if your host
raises the quota. Nobody has to press anything.

== Expiry ==

Every container has an expiry date. When it passes, the container is stopped
and its SSH login is disabled. NOTHING IS DELETED — expiry is a switch, not
a broom. Ask your host to renew; moving the date forward brings it straight
back, same data, same login.

The date is on the login screen every time you come in, and `dashboard box`
prints how many days are left.

== What is not limited ==

There is no limit here on what you may run, and no inspection of it. What
there is: your container cannot change the machine's network, cannot see any
other container, and cannot take the machine down. Those are the walls, and
they are the reason the rest of it is up to you.
